Five Books

These five interconnected books follow a deliberate conceptual sequence. Each develops the groundwork for the next, moving from temporality and subjectivity through political economy and postcolonial theory, and culminating in a theoretical memoir about race, trauma, and Indo-Fijian diasporic experience.

1. Retrofuturism

Retrofuturism begins from the proposition that the past comes from the future. Emerging from my work on Kant and spontaneity, the book considers how the past is not simply left behind but continually constituted and transformed by the possibilities through which we approach it.

2. Death of the Phallus

Death of the Phallus is a rejoinder to Joan Copjec’s Read My Desire: Lacan Against the Historicists. It argues for the irreducible singularity of the self over and against the quasi-universality of the subject and language.

Taking up Derrida’s formulation—“I have only one language; it is not mine”—the book asks how a singular self inhabits a linguistic structure that precedes and exceeds it. In developing this problem, it defends important dimensions of Foucault’s project while reinterpreting Lacan and the political and emancipatory possibilities of psychoanalysis.

3. Marx and His Readers

Marx and His Readers presents my interpretation of Marx, political economy, and the history of Marxist interpretation. It examines not only Marx’s work but also the competing traditions that have claimed, revised, and contested his intellectual legacy.

The book asks how Marx should be read today, how political economy constitutes social and political life, and how the history of Marxism has both clarified and obscured Marx’s original intervention.

4. The Other Jew

The Other Jew examines the Indian diaspora through the aporias of race, sovereignty, economy, and psychic life. It draws together the accounts of subjectivity and political economy developed in the preceding books.

The book asks how diasporic peoples are situated within inherited racial and political categories—and how those categories are themselves produced by particular conceptions of economy, sovereignty, and the psyche. It serves as a bridge between the theoretical arguments of the earlier books and the more personal investigation undertaken in Indopessimism.

5. Indopessimism

Indopessimism is a theoretical memoir and a rejoinder to Frank Wilderson’s Afropessimism. It problematizes received conceptions of race, class, identity, subjectivity, and political engagement from an Indo-Fijian diasporic perspective.

The book proceeds along two intersecting tracks: nihilism, approached through Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, and trauma, approached through Freud and Lacan. Bringing philosophical argument into conversation with personal and inherited experience, Indopessimism asks what forms of political thought become possible when neither identity nor emancipation can be taken for granted.
