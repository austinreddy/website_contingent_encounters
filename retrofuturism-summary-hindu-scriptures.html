Retrofuturism: A Summary of the Hindu Scriptures

A summary of the Hindu Scriptures:

Hinduism doesn't have a canon. The idea of canonicity was the ecumenical council's response to Marcion, who had invented the first Christian canon. Prior to that, not even Christianity had a canon. Technically, Hinduism is an oral tradition and an umbrella term for a family practice, which is to say, esoteric cults of the father.

But, for the most part, the shared canon amongst Hindu sects:

  1. The Vedas
  2. The Puranas
  3. The Upanishads
  4. The Ramayana
  5. The Mahabharata (where the Bhagavad Gita is from)

The Vedas are hymns to the old gods, Indra and Agni. They are the thunder and fire gods, respectively. They're revered as the source of all power and the natural forces which constitute the world. They're sung to signify "sacramental" functions, to use that phrase, namely, at births, funerals, weddings, etc.

The Puranas are commentaries to the Vedas. Enough said.

The Upanishads are commentaries to the commentaries, basically. They take place after the Mahabharata, which is when the celestial civil war between Devas and Asuras has spilled over into a yuug-cycle, leading to the death of everybody. In heaven, King Yama is having a panic attack. King Yama's job is to judge immortal souls and choose what their next life will be (King Yama from Dragon Ball Z does the same!), and, well, he's having a panic attack because everybody died and now there are too many souls! His servant has Yama recount to him the way the celestial economy works, both as a way to calm his master and also as a way for the audience to have a glimpse of an account of the divine justice that orders and structures the universe.

The Ramayana is a story that takes place in the past in heaven. Shiva, the lord of power, is willing to give power to anybody. He is not like the other devas; therefore, as an independent agent of power! All of the asuras, who are the exiled members of heaven and the rivals to the Devas, still worship Shiva and are therefore able to get boons of power from him! One such Asura is able to accumulate so much power that he becomes a demon and starts terrorizing humanity! The onslaught is so disturbing that Shiva approaches Vishnu in his heaven and says, hey buddy, I went a bit too far with this one, and Brahma, the creator god, might end up finding this so disturbing that he might wake up, ending the dream and subsequently the universe! What shall we do?! Vishnu says, "In this time of a-dharma, I will incarnate as a righteous man, Lord Rama, who will uphold the dharma, to show the humans what justice, kindness, mercy and courage look like; to show them the meaning of dharma, the meaning of this world!" The story recounts the birth of Rama, his marriage to Sita, his conflict with Ravana, and his subsequent exile until his return as the next avatar in the next yuug cycle! The Ramayana therefore depicts the story of the ideal life and the beginning of the messianic aspiration in the history of religion!

The Mahabharata is another story that takes place in the next yuug cycle. Essentially, this one is a complicated political drama that takes place between Dhuryodon, who is functioning as the King of the empire, and his cousins, the five pandava brothers: Yudhisthira, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva, who are all married to Draupadi because of an accident, who are also the legitimate heirs to the throne, or at least kinda! It gets very complicated by the end! However, through this familial civil war, the celestial civil war continues and is recruited by both sides into a world-ending catastrophe, and also Brahma narrates the personal civil war for each individual in the battle for good and evil in the soul! This story also features the scene depicted as the Bhagavad Gita, which is the revelation of Lord Krishna as the next incarnation of Vishnu, and his song recounts to Arjuna, who is depressed at the time when he sees his former mentors and beloved teachers on the other side of the battlefield against him, on the nature of war, conflict, the soul, the divine, morality and duty! Krishna urges him to have hope, to see beyond the antagonisms of the present age the eternal and enduring dharma, and to have faith and trust in God, that is, to trust in himself, that this is all being carried out for a purpose!

Important theological points about the dispensations/covenants of God.

When Abraham is called from Canaan, this is meant to be a "normative inversion" of the Canaanite beliefs. However, the Hebrew Bible establishes a continuity between this first "Canaanite" or "pre-Israelite" dispensation/covenant and the second Abrahamic covenant; the historical-critical view of this is that the ancient Israelite religion is a byproduct of the early Canaanite religions, but this is even attested in the scripture's narrative of itself when Abraham, before leaving Canaan, gets the blessing from the high priest, King Malchizadek, which establishes the continuity between the two religions (despite the later Mosaic distinction, even, btw) and also which the apostle Paul himself will draw on as evidence for why Christ's new covenant is in continuity with the old religion ("for I have come to fulfill the law, not abolish it," which is essentially the kernel of retrofutural truth in the gospel!).
